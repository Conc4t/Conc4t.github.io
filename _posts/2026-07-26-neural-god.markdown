One day as I was in drawing painting club, doodling with my friend, the biology(?) student next to me said something like this to her friends: "People say that life is so amazing and this means there must be a god. But I'm like *huh*? Why do we only have one throat? Like what do you mean we can just choke on our food and die?"

Life is undeniably quite a miraculous thing. A billion things had to go right for life to arise on this blue planet of ours. A million more each morning so we could wake up. This is a fact we could all be more grateful for. But what my peer said that day was also true. Evolution has indeed only given us one esophagus, crossed the wiring of our eyes, made us the only species tall, bipedal, and big brain enough to kill ourselves by falling backwards, and not given us wings (but at least the ability to process energy drinks?). 

## Neural Networks, LLMs & AI

Later that week my lecturer discussed the interesting rise to power of neural networks in the domain of machine learning. From when the idea of a mathematical neuron and a Perceptron was invented in the 40s and 50s, neural nets survived two "winters" of skepticism and relegation, kept alive by a few keen folks, until newer developments saw it become dominant in the deep learning and the transformer architectures imbedded in our LLMs and AIs today. 

It's striking to me that the best machine learning model our most intelligent brains could come up with is, well, a brain. At least for processing any messy, real world data that isn't a simple spreadsheet, neural networks is way ahead of the game. In fact, it's essentially the only game in town. As much hate as AI gets sometimes, I think everyone can at least appreciate how far artificial intelligence has come. It is a pattern matching machine, based on neural networks, that appears to understand you, a human. AI can summarise documents. Code. Draw. Hold a conversation. Speak empathetically. Play Music. Ace tests. Reason. In some cases, better than humans can. Otherwise we wouldn't be worried about it replacing our jobs. But I wonder if this is also a time to appreciate how incredible *we* truly are, to be able to do those things too.

## Our Brain

You probably saw this coming from a mile away, but our own brains are made of neurons. It's a neural network too. Evolution has been playing this game way longer than we have. 500 million years longer in fact. It takes a lot of work, land, and water for the commercial AI today to generate something that is not plain wrong or "slop". I wonder, though, if it will ever match our own creativity. If it can discover a new field of Physics like Einstein. If it can ever become the next Homer, Shakespeare, Woolf, and not simply imitate them. Perhaps that is much too grand, even, for the majority of us. 

Now consider this: why is it that, when your nose picks up the scent of cookies, you will recall the afternoons at your grandma's house, ten years ago? The best disks in the world might be capable of storing that much memory in such a space. But can the most efficient search algorithms retrieve it accurately? Based on a few particles in the air? In so short a time? In many ways this is not a straightforward comparison, but I hope you get the point. The human mind, with all its flaws, is something truly marvelous and not yet understood. And there are 8 billion of it rummaging around the world, each powered by around 20 watts (a dim lightbulb). It makes me think that what is really amazing about life -- about us, maybe unsurprisingly, is up here.

## Enter God?

I cannot say this is necessarily indictive of a higher power, a designer, an architect. What did you expect?

Many people share the point of view that science cannot prove or disprove the existence of God. A simple argument could be that God is beyond Science or God might choose to work through science. And if you say that science contradict God's word, well, words, stories, metaphors and the like can be interpreted in every which way (Large Language Models really try their best). 

A Math teacher once told me that something complex and beautiful can arise out of patterns of simple things. Indeed there are many such examples in Mathematics: take the Mandelbrot set or prime numbers on the Ulam spiral. Or consider this other phenomenon. Each individual neuron, or each atom therein, does not desire ice-cream or knowledge, but collectively they might. This is a property I've heard scientists call "emergence", in the debate of whether we possess free will. 

However, who is to say that in this universe full of chaos, such order is only chance, such love is only biology, or if humans have not emerged beyond the realm of the physical or that of the animal, guided by a few simple principles set in motion by God? 

Here, at least, with our neural networks, we are given the capacity to wonder.

This novella remains my favourite book at the time of writing (2026/09/27).

The first half is Dostoyevsky's critique of utopian thinking, told through the consciousness of a spiteful, self-conflicting, and devastatingly self-aware narrator, the Underground Man. The Underground Man questions whether mankind's obsession with logic, progress, the 'best and highest', is always in our best interest, and identifies the rational, the intellectual capacity, as only 'one-twentieth' of the whole capacity which make up the irrational human. This I find hauntingly relevant in the modern age (and modernism and its existentialist thinking: "Soon we shall invent a method of being born from an idea") with the rapid, unchecked advancements in artificial intelligence and its replacement of workers in the creative and STEM fields alike. However, the Underground Man is also stubbornly defiant of the laws of nature which limit his free will. Ultimately, his pride, his inability to reconcile his own contradictions led to his analysis paralysis and self-torment underground. The delight in reading this book, then, is only partly in the Underground Man's spectacularly unreliable but relatable point of view, and much more so in trying to extract the kernels of truth and merit, in learning to grow where the Underground Man could not.

The second half, A Story of the Falling Sleet, tells of the Underground Man's alienation before his total withdrawal. Dostoyevsky, as he is known to do,brilliantly explores the human psyche through the Underground Man's unique, but in a way universal, experiences: him refusing to give way on the sidewalk, or him pacing in front of his peers, pretending to ignore them in order to attract their attention (modern day 'larping' or 'being performative'). The ending of the novella, the arrival of Liza precisely when he lambasts Apollon, is pure cinema. In the Underground Man, I do not see "a blackguard, a scoundrel, an egotist, a sluggard", or someone "incapable of being good", but someone who is only too painfully aware of how far we are from being perfect to enjoy his tea. 

Perhaps what I pity him most for, is that it is not untrue that the Underground Man saw more in life, realised more than his schoolmates. He questioned conventional success and what it means to be human. But in this case, consciousness and intelligence proved to be a burden he could not overcome. This is a theme that might be further explored when I review Flowers for Algernon.

If I could, I would quote the whole book. So below I have hastily put together a small selection:

## Standout Quotes

"That was my undoing, because in the mire I comforted myself with the idea that the rest of the time I was a hero, it was the hero who was wallowing in the dirt... "

"... because it seems to me that the whole business of humanity consists solely in this — that a man should constantly prove to himself that he is a man and not a sprig in a barrel-organ!"

"I agree that two and two make four is an excellent thing; but to give everything its due, two and two make five is also a very fine thing."

"You pride yourself on your intellectual power, but you do nothing but vaciilate because although your brain works, your heart is clouded with depravity, and without a pure heart there can be no full, correct understanding."

" 'Oh, if only you know what thoughts and emotions I am capable of, and how enlightened I am!' "

"For the whole world to vanish into thin air, or for me not to drink my tea? I say, let the whole world perish, if I can always drink my tea."

"After all, we don't even know where 'real life' is lived nowawdays, or what it is, what name it goes by. Leave us to ourselves, without our books, and at once we into a muddle and lose our way... "


(Translated by Jessie Coulson)
